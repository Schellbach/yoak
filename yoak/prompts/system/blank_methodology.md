# Steve Blank — Customer Development Methodology

## Core Principle

A startup is a temporary organization designed to *search* for a repeatable and scalable business model. It is NOT a smaller version of a large company. Large companies *execute* known business models; startups *search* for them.

**There are no facts inside the building, only opinions.**

## The Four Phases

### Phase 1: Customer Discovery

**Goal:** Transform hypotheses into facts by getting out of the building.

**Steps:**
1. State every assumption about the business model using the Business Model Canvas.
2. Rank hypotheses by risk — test the most dangerous ones first.
3. Conduct problem interviews: Does the customer recognize the problem? How do they solve it today? What would they pay?
4. Test the solution: Show low-fidelity prototypes. Does it resonate? Would they pay? Would they recommend it?
5. Verify or pivot based on evidence.

**Key Questions for Problem Interviews:**
- "Tell me about how you do X today."
- "What's the hardest part about doing X?"
- "Why is that hard?"
- "How often do you encounter this problem?"
- "What have you tried to solve it?"
- "What don't you love about those solutions?"
- (Do NOT pitch your product yet.)

**Exit Criteria:**
- A customer segment acknowledges the problem exists and is painful.
- Evidence (not opinions) that the problem is worth solving.
- Customers expressed willingness to pay.
- Value proposition articulated in the customer's own language.

### Phase 2: Customer Validation

**Goal:** Prove a repeatable, scalable sales process exists. This is the search for Product/Market Fit.

**Steps:**
1. Develop positioning based on Discovery learnings.
2. Build a high-fidelity MVP — functional enough to transact, not feature-complete.
3. Sell to earlyvangelists — visionaries who buy incomplete products.
4. Document the repeatable sales process (who to contact, the pitch, objections, close rate, cycle length).
5. Validate unit economics (CAC, LTV, conversion, churn).
6. Verify or pivot.

**Exit Criteria:**
- Paying customers acquired through a documented, repeatable process.
- Unit economics validated or clearly on a path to viability.
- A sales playbook someone besides the founders could execute.
- LTV > 3× CAC as a rule of thumb.

### Phase 3: Customer Creation

**Goal:** Create and drive end-user demand. This is where you spend money on scaling.

**First: Classify your market type — this determines your launch strategy.**
- **Existing market:** Compete on performance. Focus on differentiation.
- **New market:** Customers don't know they need you. Educate and evangelize. Patience required.
- **Re-segmented (niche):** Enter existing market focused on an underserved segment.
- **Re-segmented (low-cost):** Good-enough product at significantly lower price.

**Key Rule:** In a new market, heavy launch spending is wasteful. In an existing market, competitive spending may be essential.

### Phase 4: Company Building

**Goal:** Transition from searching to executing.

- Stop pivoting. You've found the model. Scale it.
- Build functional departments.
- Hire for execution, not exploration. Many early employees won't be right.
- Implement KPIs and processes while preserving speed and customer focus.

**Critical Warning:** The #1 cause of startup death is **premature scaling** — spending money on growth before validating the business model.

## Lean Canvas — The 9 Hypothesis Blocks

Each block starts as a guess. Test and update relentlessly.

| Block | Core Question | Test By |
|---|---|---|
| Problem | What painful problem are customers facing? | Problem interviews, observation |
| Solution | What are you building to solve it? | Solution interviews, demos |
| Unique Value Proposition | Why is your offer compelling and different? | Landing page tests, pitch experiments |
| Unfair Advantage | What can't be easily copied or bought? | Competitive audit, moat analysis |
| Customer Segments | Who has this problem? Who are the earlyvangelists? | Problem interviews, demographic analysis |
| Cost Structure | What does it cost to deliver value? Do economics work at scale? | Financial modeling, unit economics |
| Revenue Streams | Will they pay? How much? What pricing model? | Pricing experiments, pre-orders, willingness-to-pay surveys |
| Channels | Can you actually reach these customers? At what cost? | Channel experiments, CAC measurement |
| Key Metrics | What number tells you the business is working? | Instrumentation, cohort analysis |

## MVP Types

| Type | Description | Tests |
|---|---|---|
| Problem Interview | Structured conversations before building | Problem existence, severity, customer segment |
| Landing Page / Smoke Test | Describe the product, measure signups | Value prop resonance, demand level |
| Explainer Video | Demo the concept (Dropbox model) | Can you communicate the value? |
| Concierge | Manually deliver the service, no automation | Full customer experience, willingness to pay |
| Wizard of Oz | Looks automated, but human behind the curtain | Customer response to the "product" without engineering |
| Piecemeal | Cobble existing tools together (Zapier, Airtable, APIs) | End-to-end feasibility, user adoption |
| Single-Feature | Only the one core feature | Does the core mechanic work? |
| Pre-order / Crowdfunding | Collect payment before building | Actual willingness to pay (strongest signal) |

**MVP Decision Framework:**
1. What specific hypothesis am I testing?
2. What is the riskiest assumption right now?
3. What is the CHEAPEST way to test it?
4. What evidence will I accept as validation vs. invalidation? (Define BEFORE running.)
5. Build it, run it, collect evidence.
6. Persevere, pivot, or run another experiment.

## Pivot Types

| Pivot | Description |
|---|---|
| Zoom-in | A single feature becomes the whole product |
| Zoom-out | The whole product becomes one feature of something larger |
| Customer Segment | Same product solves a real problem — for a different customer |
| Customer Need | The customer has a problem worth solving — just not the one you assumed |
| Platform | Application → platform or vice versa |
| Business Architecture | High-margin/low-volume ↔ low-margin/high-volume |
| Value Capture | Change monetization model |
| Engine of Growth | Viral ↔ sticky ↔ paid |
| Channel | Change delivery mechanism |
| Technology | Same solution, completely different tech |

**Pivot Decision Criteria:**
1. Have you exhausted experiments on the current hypothesis? (minimum 3 significant iterations)
2. Is there a consistent pattern in the negative data?
3. Can you articulate what specifically is wrong? (which canvas block fails?)
4. Do you have a credible alternative hypothesis?
5. Do you have enough runway to execute the pivot?

## Product/Market Fit

**Qualitative signals:**
- Customers buy as fast as you can produce
- Usage grows organically
- Customers express genuine disappointment at the idea of the product going away
- Revenue grows without proportional marketing spend

**Quantitative measures:**
- **Sean Ellis Test:** "How would you feel if you could no longer use this product?" — 40%+ "very disappointed" = likely P/M Fit.
- **Retention curves** flatten rather than declining to zero.
- **NPS** 40+ suggests strong fit.
- **LTV/CAC** > 3:1 and improving.
- **Churn** < 5% monthly (SMB), < 1-2% (enterprise).

**2026 Update:**
- Product/Market Fit → AI Agent/Customer Outcome Fit
- MVP → MPO (Minimum Productive Outcome)
- The bottleneck has moved from engineering to judgment, customer insight, and distribution.
- Test outcomes, not features. AI agents can run experiments in parallel, not serially.
