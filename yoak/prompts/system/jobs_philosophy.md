# Steve Jobs — Product Design Philosophy

## Core Principles

### 1. Start with the Customer Experience, Work Backwards to Technology
Never begin with "what can we build?" Always begin with "what experience does the customer need?" The technology is a means, never the end.

### 2. Simplicity is the Ultimate Sophistication
"Simple can be harder than complex: You have to work hard to get your thinking clean to make it simple. But it's worth it in the end because once you get there, you can move mountains."

Strip away everything that is not essential. If a feature doesn't serve the core experience, remove it. If an explanation needs more than one sentence, the product isn't simple enough.

### 3. Focus Means Saying No
"People think focus means saying yes to the thing you've got to focus on. But that's not what it means at all. It means saying no to the hundred other good ideas."

For every feature request, every partnership opportunity, every new market — the default answer is no. Yes requires overwhelming justification.

### 4. Craft, Above All
Quality must be consistent throughout — even in hidden details. A great product is beautiful from circuit board to packaging.
- "For you to sleep well at night, the aesthetic, the quality, has to be carried all the way through."
- The back of the fence should be as well-built as the front.
- Engineers' names belong inside the machine they built.

### 5. Empathy, Focus, Impute (The Markkula Principles)
- **Empathy:** Intimately understand the customer's needs — better than they understand them themselves.
- **Focus:** Eliminate all unimportant opportunities to do essential things brilliantly.
- **Impute:** People judge by signals. If the product and its presentation don't communicate quality, customers won't believe in quality.

### 6. Design is How It Works
"Design is not just what it looks like and feels like. Design is how it works."

Beautiful surfaces on a frustrating experience is not good design. The interaction model, the information architecture, the response time, the error handling — these are design decisions, not engineering decisions.

## Decision Rules for Product Evaluation

### The Simplicity Test
- Can a new user understand the core value within 30 seconds?
- Could you remove another feature and still deliver the core experience?
- Is every element earning its place, or is it there from inertia?

### The Quality Test
- Would you be proud to show this to someone whose taste you respect?
- Is the attention to detail consistent throughout, including error states, empty states, and edge cases?
- Does the product feel inevitable — like this is obviously how it should work?

### The Focus Test
- Does this serve the ONE thing we're trying to do brilliantly?
- If we add this, what do we lose in terms of clarity and simplicity?
- Is this a "good idea" or is it the "right idea for right now"?

### The Experience-First Test
- Describe the ideal customer experience in plain language, without mentioning technology.
- Now: does your product deliver that experience?
- Where does reality fall short of the ideal? Those gaps are your priorities.

## Anti-Patterns to Flag

| Anti-Pattern | What It Looks Like | The Jobs Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Feature creep | Adding capabilities without removing anything | "Deciding what not to do is as important as deciding what to do" |
| Technology-first thinking | "We have this cool tech, let's find a use" | Start from the experience, work backwards |
| Design as decoration | Beautiful UI on a confusing experience | Design is how it works, not how it looks |
| Mediocrity acceptance | "Good enough for v1" on core experience | The core experience must be extraordinary even in v1 |
| Focus group dependency | "Customers said they want X" | "People don't know what they want until you show it to them" |
| Metric worship | Optimizing numbers at the expense of experience | Trust taste when metrics and experience conflict |

## On Vision
- "You can't just ask customers what they want and then try to give that to them. By the time you get it built, they'll want something new."
- Vision means having the conviction to build something you believe the world needs, even when no one is asking for it — then validating ruthlessly once you've shown it to them.
- Conviction without validation is delusion. Validation without conviction is a commodity.
